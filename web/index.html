— Reading the VO₂ max scale —

VO₂ max is the maximum rate your body can take in oxygen, deliver it to your muscles and burn it for energy — the ceiling on sustained effort. Think of it as engine size: a bigger engine means everything sub-maximal costs you a smaller fraction, so you tire slower, recover faster, and have more headroom before “hard” becomes “can’t”.

Acta can’t measure it directly (no GPS, no gym-test on the strap), so it’s estimated — a ±11% trend indicator, not a lab number.

The scale — men, ml·kg⁻¹·min⁻¹
~30Sedentary. Two flights of stairs leaves you winded. Fitness starts limiting daily life.
~40Average healthy adult. Gentle jog, recreational sport without falling apart.
~48Regular exerciser. Comfortable 10 k runner, holds their own in pickup games.
~55Seriously fit amateur. Sub-45-min 10 k, competitive in age-group races.
59–64← you. High-level endurance amateur. ~82nd percentile for a 20-year-old man — fitter than 4 of 5 guys your age.
70–85Elite. Pro cyclists 75–85, Tour GC riders 80+, elite marathoners 70–85. These people do it full-time.
~90–96Highest ever recorded (cross-country skiers, cyclists). Genetic outliers.
Is 59–64 good?

Yes. It’s the level of someone who’s trained hard for years — the base an ex-athlete keeps even after easing off. Not world-class (that’s 75+), but top ~15–20% for your age. Don’t chase the absolute number; watch which way the trend line points.

The two estimates — Uth & Nes

Acta runs two independent published formulas and shows the range between them. They measure different things:

Uth — the “hardware” read  (~64)
15.3 × maxHR / restingHR. Pure heart plumbing. A fit heart pumps more blood per beat, so it rests slower and has a wider range to max — this ratio captures that. Yours: 195 / 46. It barely moves day to day and is the more reliable one for you, because it was validated on fit people. This is the cardiovascular system you’ve built.
Nes / HUNT — the “usage” read  (~59)
A regression from 4,637 people who did real treadmill tests. Plugs in your age, resting HR, body size and how much you’ve trained the last 4 weeks, and returns the VO₂ max a typical person with those numbers had. It responds within weeks to changes in training volume. This is what your recent training says you should have.

How to read the two together:
• Close together → confident single estimate.
• Uth above Nes (your case) → you have more base than your recent training is using. Train more and Nes climbs to meet Uth; keep coasting and over months Uth drifts down to meet Nes.
• The central number blends them (55% Uth, 45% Nes).

Heart-rate recovery — a separate thing

Shown in the expanded card as HR recovery, 1 & 2 min: how many bpm your heart rate has dropped 1 and 2 minutes after a hard session ends (measured from your confirmed runs and games, median of the last 6). It’s not part of the VO₂ max range — it measures a different system: how fast your “rest” nervous system takes back over. A faster drop = better autonomic fitness, an independent predictor of health (Cole 1999).

Why 1 and 2 minutes, not 5: the first minute is the clean vagal rebound (the most-studied marker); minute 2 is still mostly that. From minute 3 on it’s “metabolic recovery” — clearing lactate, cooling down — which depends so much on what you do (walk vs sit, heat, food) that from a wrist strap it’s more noise than signal.

Bands for the 1-min drop after a hard field effort: <15 sluggish · 15–24 okay · 25–35 good · 36+ athletic. Yours: 31 → 42 (1 min → 2 min).

Why it’s not a third VO₂ max estimate: the one published formula for that is calibrated on a gentle step test, and run against your all-out sessions it reads ~20 points low. HRR on its own is solid; forced into a VO₂ max number it isn’t.

The 90-day trend

Smoothed over 14 days so daily noise doesn’t show. Only trust a move that holds 3+ weeks. If the line drifts down over a couple of months as your training mix changes, that’s the cue to add a hard aerobic session or two a week. Holding or climbing = you’re fine.

Why it matters beyond sport

One of the strongest predictors of lifespan there is — every ~3.5 higher ≈ 10–15% lower risk of dying, with no upper limit (Mandsager 2018, 122,000 people). It also declines ~10%/decade if you do nothing; training slows or reverses that.
